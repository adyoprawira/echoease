## Hypothesis 1: Students already have an internal expectation of where a Support & Well‑being button should appear on Blackboard, and would describe a consistent location and purpose.
[TestCard1](https://github.com/COMP1100-2026-s1/54_Richard/blob/main/Documents/Iteration%202/MVP_test_cards.md#test-card-1-support-button-discoverability)

**HYPOTHESIS**  
We believed that students already have an internal expectation of where a Support & Well‑being button should appear on Blackboard, and they would describe a consistent location and purpose.

**OBSERVATIONS**  
We observed that while students are aware of centralized university services, they primarily use them for administrative or academic issues.  
For high-stress situations (e.g., mental health or financial crises), students perceived these services as too formal and restricted by office hours, making them inaccessible when stress peaks (e.g., late at night).

**LEARNING AND INSIGHTS**  
Students share a clear and consistent placement expectation, which reduces cognitive load.
However, the emotional mismatch between “academic platform” and “wellbeing support” creates hesitation.
Discoverability is not only visual, it requires contextual justification to feel natural. 

**DECISIONS AND ACTIONS**  
Therefore, we will accept this hypothesis.

## Hypothesis 2: Students can infer the purpose of the wellbeing landing page from the three option labels
[TestCard2](https://github.com/COMP1100-2026-s1/54_Richard/blob/main/Documents/Iteration%202/MVP_test_cards.md#test-card-2-landing-page-clarity--first-impressions)

**HYPOTHESIS**  
We believed that students could understand the purpose of the wellbeing landing page based solely on the three option labels.

**OBSERVATIONS**  
Students correctly interpreted the three options; Community Stories, Chat with Someone, and Emergency Resources without needing additional explanation.
They mapped each option to a distinct type of support and described the layout as “clear” and “understandable.”
First-click preferences varied depending on emotional state (stories for reassurance, chat for stress, emergency for anxiety).

**LEARNING AND INSIGHTS**  
The labels align well with students’ existing mental models of support.
Clarity is strong, but different students prioritise different pathways, suggesting the value of personalisation.
Short descriptions and icons further strengthen comprehension.

**DECISIONS AND ACTIONS**  
Therefore, we will accept this hypothesis.

## Hypothesis 3: Students will perceive an anonymous community as a safe, low‑pressure way to understand others’ experiences  
[TestCard3](https://github.com/COMP1100-2026-s1/54_Richard/blob/main/Documents/Iteration%202/MVP_test_cards.md#test-card-3-anonymous-community-engagement)

**HYPOTHESIS**  
We believed that students would feel comfortable reading anonymous posts and find value in seeing others’ experiences.

**OBSERVATIONS**  
Students described anonymous browsing as comfortable and relatable, comparing it to “reading Reddit.”
Community stories reduced feelings of isolation and provided emotional reassurance.
However, students expressed concerns about heavy emotional content, privacy, and emotional contagion.
Most preferred the option to reply, but only if anonymity was guaranteed.

**LEARNING AND INSIGHTS**  
Anonymity is the foundation of emotional safety.
Students want connection, but only in a moderated, supportive environment.
Read‑only browsing is safe, but optional interaction increases value when anonymity is preserved.

**DECISIONS AND ACTIONS**  
Therefore, we will accept this hypothesis.

## Hypothesis 4: Students will find anonymous posting appealing and understand the usefulness of topic tags 
[TestCard4](https://github.com/COMP1100-2026-s1/54_Richard/blob/main/Documents/Iteration%202/MVP_test_cards.md#test-card-4-create-post-flow-peer-support-community)

**HYPOTHESIS**  
We believed that anonymity and topic tags would increase students’ willingness to share personal experiences.

**OBSERVATIONS**  
All students emphasised that anonymity is “very important” or “extremely important.”
Posting becomes easier when anonymous, but emotional vulnerability still makes sharing difficult.
Students said they would post when lonely, stressed, seeking outside opinions, or after overcoming something.
Topic tags helped them organise their thoughts and express their situation.response times were too slow.

**LEARNING AND INSIGHTS**  
The barrier to posting is emotional, not functional.
Tags act as cognitive scaffolding, helping students articulate difficult feelings.
Students need reassurance that anonymity is real and that their data is protected.

**DECISIONS AND ACTIONS**  
Therefore, we will accept this hypothesis.

## Hypothesis 5: Students will perceive a no‑booking chat as more approachable than formal counselling 
[TestCard5](https://github.com/COMP1100-2026-s1/54_Richard/blob/main/Documents/Iteration%202/MVP_test_cards.md#test-card-5-private-chat-accessibility)

**HYPOTHESIS**  
We believed that students would find a casual, no‑booking chat with a peer mentor more approachable than formal counselling.

**OBSERVATIONS**  
Students described chat as “less intimidating” and similar to “texting,” especially compared to formal appointments.
Late‑night support was highly valued.
Trust depended on qualifications, confidentiality, privacy, and response speed.
Students were cautious about slow replies or unclear data handling.

**LEARNING AND INSIGHTS**  
Students prefer human connection over automation, but trust is fragile.
Chat is the most appealing active support pathway, but only when privacy and legitimacy are clearly communicated.

**DECISIONS AND ACTIONS**  
Therefore, we will accept this hypothesis.

## Hypothesis 6: Students prefer peer-based community support over automation  
[TestCard6](https://github.com/COMP1100-2026-s1/54_Richard/blob/main/Documents/Iteration%202/MVP_test_cards.md#test-card-6-emergency-resources-visibility)

**HYPOTHESIS**  
We believed that students would expect emergency resources to be highly visible and clearly labelled.

**OBSERVATIONS**  
Students consistently expected emergency help to be at the top of the page, fixed, or always visible.
They preferred red colour cues, bold text, and simple, direct wording.
Students trusted UQ to provide correct emergency contacts but did not want to search for them during stress.
Emotional reassurance was important; wording needed to feel supportive, not clinical.

**LEARNING AND INSIGHTS**  
Crisis navigation must be zero‑friction.
Visual cues and emotional tone both influence trust and usability.
Students rely on institutional authority during emergencies.

**DECISIONS AND ACTIONS**  
Therefore, we will accept this hypothesis.
